# Principles

- Truth-seeking via formal methods
  - Favor formalization and typechecking over social proof. Claims are embodied as formal artifacts and evaluated by a checker, never by who published them.
- Validity over truth (by design)
  - The platform does not determine which axioms are true. It holds authors accountable to logical consequence: given their stated assumptions, do conclusions follow? Truth requires knowing which axioms are true; it therefore focuses on the next best thing—logical validity.
- Freedom to publish
  - The system should not require identity. It should enable complete anonymity for publishers of beliefs.
- Idea-centric evaluation
  - Anonymity removes the ability to invoke identity politics or identity-based agreement/disagreement; acceptance depends on the quality of ideas, evidence, and formal validity.
- Philosophy and self-discovery
  - The platform invites the search for axioms that generate one’s worldview. By making assumptions explicit, the practice of formalization becomes a philosophical exercise—often revealing not just conclusions, but the self behind them.
- Resilience to suppression
  - Knowledge should remain available even if individual venues disappear. The network should degrade gracefully under coercion or takedowns.
- Minimal coordination
  - Avoid centralized gatekeepers. Anyone should be able to host a registry and mirror content without permission.
- Interoperability through simplicity
  - Keep the protocol minimal and transparent, so registries and clients can be implemented and audited easily.
